## Evidence over opinion

I am ADsP certified in data analytics and I use it. On the exchange project I read session data before touching a screen, and it said people left in 45 seconds — which meant the problem was not the button the team was arguing about. In hospitality I ran research during live shifts, because what people say about their work and what their work looks like are different things.

## The design system is the handoff

I build three-tier token architectures — primitive, semantic, component — and document them in writing, not only in Figma.

That matters more than it used to. A written system is readable by three audiences at once: a developer, a design tool, and an AI coding agent. When the specification is precise, all three produce the same thing.

## Constraints first, then the experience

I find out what a product is not allowed to do before I decide what it should do. Accessibility standards, data protection, whatever the record has to prove — these are inputs, not obstacles discovered at sign-off.

Two reasons. Constraints found late get solved badly, because by then the cheapest fix is a warning message bolted onto a finished flow. And knowing the rules tells me where I am free: if I know which three fields carry legal weight, the other forty can be as fast and light as the user deserves.

I would rather bring a design to a risk review already knowing where its exposure is than have someone else find it for me.

## Where AI belongs, and where it does not

I build hands-on with Claude Code, Cursor and Figma MCP.

**Good at:** turning a documented system into on-system code · collapsing the distance between a decision and something testable · producing the fifth and sixth variation so a critique has real material · writing documentation nobody has time for.

**Kept out of its hands:** the research and the reframe · anything with a legal consequence · accessibility trade-offs, which need judgement about when to exceed a standard and when to relax it · final visual judgement.

The failure mode I watch for is fluency. AI output looks finished before it is correct, and a plausible screen is harder to challenge in review than a rough one.

## Introducing it to an existing team

1. **Make the system machine-readable first.** Without a written specification, AI amplifies inconsistency instead of reducing it.
2. **Start where being wrong is cheapest.** Documentation, variants, prototype scaffolding — not customer-facing copy or compliance journeys.
3. **Keep a human decision point wherever there is a consequence.** A design process that cannot produce its own audit trail is not ready to be automated.
4. **Measure the review cycle, not design time.** That is where a documented system actually pays back, and it is measurable.
5. **Let it stay boring.** The goal is a team of four shipping what a team of eight used to, with fewer arguments about spacing.
